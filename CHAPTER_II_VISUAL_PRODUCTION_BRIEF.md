# DORIAN GRAY — CHAPTER II VISUAL PRODUCTION BRIEF

## Status and scope

This is a production brief for the visual assets of Chapter II — *The Actress*.

This milestone deliberately does **not**:

- generate or add any image files;
- integrate images into the application;
- modify the engine, story data, Chapter I, portrait thresholds or UI;
- create a Chapter III visual;
- show Sibyl dead, injured, poisoned, buried or otherwise reveal an outcome through art;
- create separate canonical and alternative ending images.

The approved visual direction remains **Victorian Gothic, Dark Academia, classical oil painting, candlelight, restrained antique-gold accents, and literary rather than fantasy atmosphere**, as defined in `VISUAL_BIBLE.md`.

The current application has no Chapter II image hooks. Chapter II scenes are text-driven, while the only current raster mapping is Dorian's 4:5 portrait system. The asset list below is therefore a production plan for a later integration milestone, not an instruction to change the current renderer.

## 1. Primary-text evidence and production boundary

The primary reference is Oscar Wilde's 1891 edition of *The Picture of Dorian Gray*:

- [Chapter IV — Dorian describes Sibyl, the little theatre and her stage roles](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Picture_of_Dorian_Gray_(1891)/Chapter_4)
- [Chapter V — Sibyl, Mrs Vane, Jim, money, family and the dingy sitting-room](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Picture_of_Dorian_Gray_(1891)/Chapter_5)
- [Chapter VI — Basil and Lord Henry discuss Dorian's engagement](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Picture_of_Dorian_Gray_(1891)/Chapter_6)
- [Chapter VII — the crowded theatre, Sibyl's appearance and the final performance](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Picture_of_Dorian_Gray_(1891)/Chapter_7)
- [Chapter VIII — the non-graphic canonical aftermath](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Picture_of_Dorian_Gray_(1891)/Chapter_8)

### Canon-supported facts

| Fact | Treatment in the visual system |
| --- | --- |
| Sibyl is described as “hardly seventeen” | Treat her as approximately 16–17, a minor. All imagery is fully clothed, age-appropriate and non-sexualised. |
| She has a little flower-like face and a small Greek head | Use a small, delicate, softly oval face with a clear but gentle profile. The exact geometry below is a production decision, not a claim that Wilde supplied a measurement. |
| Her eyes are described as violet | Use muted violet eyes as a stable identity landmark, never glowing or fantasy-bright. |
| Her hair is dark brown and arranged in plaited coils | Use dark brown hair; stage styling may form controlled braided coils, while off-stage styling is simpler and less arranged. |
| She has shy grace, startled eyes and a faint blush | Preserve a shy, attentive, easily startled expression; do not replace it with adult glamour or melodramatic sadness. |
| She is shy and gentle off stage and Dorian first understands her through roles | The stage/off-stage distinction is contextual: performed image versus real person, not beautiful versus unattractive. |
| She lives with her mother and brother in financially difficult circumstances | The Vane environment is modest, worn and practical, never a poverty caricature or sentimental period tableau. |
| She performs Juliet, Rosalind, Imogen and other heroines | Stage images may vary costume and lighting, but her face, hair colour, eye colour and proportions remain fixed. |
| The final performance is connected to Dorian's disappointment | Shared assets must remain neutral enough to serve every route and must not foreshadow death, separation or a future portrait state. |

### Controlled production decisions

The 1891 text does not specify a complete turn-around model, exact height, exact face proportions, a single everyday hairstyle, a camera position, an image palette or a specific theatre architecture. Those details are locked here solely to keep future images consistent.

The older language used in the source text is not copied into prompts or alt text. The visual brief uses neutral, respectful descriptions of a young Victorian actress and her family.

## 2. Sibyl Vane — canonical visual identity lock

### Identity anchor

Sibyl Vane is a young Victorian stage actress of approximately 16–17 years of age. She is a minor and must be depicted as such: fully clothed, age-appropriate, non-sexualised, and free of adult fashion, erotic posing, exaggerated cosmetics or suggestive framing.

| Attribute | Fixed specification | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Age | Approximately 16–17; clearly a late adolescent, not an adult woman and not a small child | Primarily text-based, with exact numeric range fixed for production |
| Face | Small softly oval face, delicate chin, gently defined cheeks, compact classical profile, expressive rather than maturely glamorous | The “small” and “flower-like” quality is text-based; the precise oval geometry is production control |
| Eyes | Muted violet, large and attentive, capable of a startled look; natural, not luminous | Violet is text-based; saturation and rendering are production choices |
| Hair | Dark brown, naturally thick, centre or soft middle part; braided coils or a controlled pinned arrangement on stage; simple braid or low pinned style off stage | Dark brown and plaited coils are text-based; exact arrangement is production control |
| Skin | Warm ivory with a natural rose flush; visible human texture, no porcelain finish | The blush is text-supported; palette and texture are production choices |
| Build | Slight, youthful and graceful, with narrow natural proportions; no adult pin-up silhouette and no fragile fantasy anatomy | Youthful delicacy follows the text; body specification is a safety and continuity control |
| Posture | Slightly inward and attentive off stage; more open and composed when performing; never posed as a fashion model | Contextual production choice |
| Characteristic expression | Shy gentleness, quick wonder, a faint blush, and a capacity for sudden concentration when performing | Grounded in the text's shyness, gentleness and startled grace |
| Clothing | High-necked, long-sleeved Victorian dresses with opaque fabric; muted plum, brown, faded blue, cream or charcoal; stage garments may be richer but remain period-accurate | Production choice; no revealing neckline, sheer fabric or modern silhouette |

### Stable recognition landmarks

Every Sibyl image must preserve these landmarks:

1. small softly oval face with a delicate chin;
2. muted violet eyes with a wide, attentive gaze;
3. dark-brown hair and a consistent hairline;
4. the same small nose and gentle mouth;
5. a natural rose flush rather than heavy stage cosmetics;
6. slight youthful shoulders and graceful, non-sexualised posture;
7. a restrained expression that can move from shy wonder to focused performance without changing identity.

Do not identify her by a beauty mark, scar, jewellery item or theatrical prop that the source text does not provide. The stable identity is her face, eyes, hair, age-appropriate proportions and expression.

## 3. On-stage versus off-stage Sibyl

The key visual contrast is:

> **PERFORMED IMAGE versus REAL PERSON**

It is not “beautiful versus ordinary”, “rich versus poor”, or “adult glamour versus childishness”. Sibyl is the same young person in both contexts.

### On stage

- warm gaslight and concentrated theatre contrast;
- costume appropriate to the role, with sleeves and a high neckline;
- carefully arranged braided hair that still shows the same hairline and dark-brown colour;
- restrained period stage cosmetics, never modern makeup;
- a more open chest and stronger theatrical gesture, but no sexualised pose;
- focused eyes and a role-specific expression;
- richer wine, cream, faded rose and deep green accents against the theatre's dark wood.

### Off stage

- softer, more directional light with more visible natural skin texture;
- simple high-necked dress or blouse and skirt in muted brown, blue, plum or cream;
- simple braid or low pinned hair with no decorative spectacle;
- quieter hands, shoulders slightly inward, and direct but uncertain attention;
- human individuality and tiredness may be present, but not ugliness, misery or caricature;
- no visual cue should imply that Sibyl is less valuable because she is not performing.

## 4. Sibyl master reference decision

### Decision: use a master reference

Create the identity anchor first and keep it outside the runtime application:

`character-sibyl-vane-MASTER.png`

The master is not a game asset and must not be placed in a UI asset map. It is the reference source for image-edit workflows and future visual review.

### Master specification

| Parameter | Specification |
| --- | --- |
| Role | Identity anchor for all Sibyl portraits and character images |
| Format | PNG, reference-only |
| Composition | Vertical 2:3, full-length or three-quarter full figure with enough face and clothing information for later edits |
| Recommended output | 1600 × 2400 px minimum |
| Clothing | Neutral high-necked late-Victorian dress in muted blue-brown or charcoal; no stage costume, no romantic jewellery |
| Hair | Dark-brown hair in a simple braid or low pinned arrangement, exposing the stable hairline and face |
| Expression | Neutral, gentle, attentive; no smile designed as seduction, no grief and no theatrical exaggeration |
| Light | Soft warm-neutral studio light with a mild cool fill; no gaslight drama that would be mistaken for the stage identity |
| Background | Plain warm grey-beige painterly background with very low detail |
| Safe area | Face, hands and upper body inside the central 70%; no important landmark within the outer 10% crop margin |
| Text policy | No lettering, title, frame, watermark, signature or logo |

### Reference workflow

1. Generate or approve the master portrait first.
2. Use the master as the identity reference for the stage and off-stage character edits.
3. Compare face shape, eye colour, hairline, mouth, age and proportions before approving any location composition.
4. Do not attempt to maintain Sibyl through repeated independent text-to-image generations.
5. If a future edit changes her age, face, eye colour or hairline, reject it even if the image is otherwise attractive.

## 6. Theatre visual identity

The theatre is small, commercially struggling and theatrically magical without being a grand opera house.

### Fixed landmarks

- dark wine or dried-burgundy main curtain, close to the project's `#703E4F` tension colour;
- shallow wooden proscenium with slightly worn gilded trim;
- gas footlights and wall gas sconces, with visible but restrained haze from the flame;
- a narrow gallery and modest side boxes rather than a monumental auditorium;
- painted scenery with visible brushwork and practical flats;
- dark, scuffed stage boards;
- a clear stage-door route leading to the backstage corridor;
- audience seating and theatre clutter that suggest ordinary work, not fantasy spectacle.

### Lighting and composition

The stage asset should put the strongest visual interest in the centre-right or right third and leave a calmer darker zone on the left or upper-left for possible story text overlays. Use warm gaslight against plum, brown and muted green. No electric spotlights, LED strips, modern rigging, microphones, metal trusses, cinema seats or contemporary signage.

## 7. Backstage visual identity

Backstage is the same building after the theatrical surface has been removed.

### Fixed landmarks

- dark painted timber and visibly joined boards;
- ropes, pulleys, canvas flats and folded scenery;
- a rack of period costumes and a practical wooden worktable;
- a small mirror or dressing table with a few historically plausible objects;
- warm gaslight falling unevenly across working surfaces;
- a visible stage-door or curtain opening that links the space to the stage;
- moderate working disorder: fabric, boxes, pins and props, but no dangerous clutter;
- enough negative space around a future character placement.

The room must feel used and human, not like a haunted basement. Avoid blood, occult symbols, broken mirrors, exaggerated shadows, cobwebs and horror-cinematic fog.

## 8. Vane family environment

`location-sibyls-home.webp` remains P1 because the implemented scenes mention the family but do not place the player inside the home.

If produced later, it should show a small, worn but cared-for Victorian sitting room: mismatched furniture, one practical armchair, a modest table, faded fabric, a window with ordinary London light, and evidence of work and debt without theatrical misery. The material contrast with Dorian's world should come through in room size, repair, light and furniture quality—not through dirt, caricature or sentimental clutter.

Mrs Vane and Jim should not be generated as separate character assets for this milestone. If they later appear in a scene image, use the same restrained, text-led identity approach and keep the family scene free of spoilers.

## 11. No spoilers through visuals

The following rules apply to every Chapter II asset:

- no dead Sibyl, body, poison, funeral, grave or post-death portrait;
- no image that visually declares alive-together or alive-estranged;
- no visible breakup, reconciliation, wedding or future family outcome;
- no new Dorian portrait stage or visible future portrait corruption;
- no Chapter III room, transformation, secret-room clue or later antagonist;
- shared assets must be usable before the final response is chosen;
- stage and off-stage identity must remain neutral enough to support every branch;
- any emotional difference must arise from context, posture and light, never from a different face or age.

The canonical death is a text-and-warning event in the current narrative design. It must never be previewed by an illustration.

## 12. Accessibility and future UI compatibility

No UI is changed in this milestone. The following requirements are for a later integration task.

### Suggested alt text

- `character-sibyl-vane-stage.webp`: “Sibyl Vane as a young Victorian stage actress in period costume under warm theatre gaslight.”
- `character-sibyl-vane-offstage.webp`: “Sibyl Vane off stage in a modest Victorian dress, looking quietly and attentively ahead.”

Alt text must describe what is visibly present, not the player's branch, Sibyl's fate or an inferred emotion. If an image is purely decorative behind already-present text, use an empty alt attribute and keep the narrative information in HTML text.

### Safe areas and image rules

- preserve the central face and hands inside the inner 70% of character images;
- preserve the left and upper-left low-detail safe zone in location assets;
- use `object-fit: contain` for future 2:3 character portraits when the full figure matters;
- use `object-fit: cover` only for location crops, with an explicit focal position;
- provide intrinsic `width` and `height` or an aspect-ratio box to prevent layout shift;
- use `loading="lazy"` for non-critical scene illustrations, but do not lazy-load an above-the-fold hero image without an explicit preload decision;
- retain the existing CSS fallback if an asset fails to load;
- test character and location crops at the existing mobile breakpoints: below 850px and below 580px.

### Current application mismatch

The existing app currently supports only Dorian's portrait image mapping and CSS fallback:

- `STORY_DATA.assets.portraitStages` is a 0/1/2 mapping for Dorian's 4:5 portrait;
- the story portrait and title portrait use `object-fit: contain` in a 4:5 box;
- `index.html` has no Chapter II image slot or scene illustration element;
- `js/story-data.js` has no visual metadata for the nine Chapter II scenes;
- `styles.css` has no character-card or location-illustration component;
- current `img` markup does not yet declare `loading`, intrinsic dimensions or Chapter II alt text.

These are recorded integration requirements only. Do not resolve them in Milestone 2D, and do not place Sibyl assets into `portraitStages`.

## 13. Identity-consistency review checklist

Before approving any future Sibyl image, compare it side by side with the master:

1. Is she still approximately 16–17 and visibly a minor?
2. Are the small oval face, delicate chin and mouth recognisable?
3. Are the eyes muted violet rather than blue, green or glowing fantasy eyes?
4. Is the hair dark brown with the same hairline?
5. Does the image preserve natural, age-appropriate proportions?
6. Does the stage/off-stage difference come from context rather than a changed face?
